## Abstract

From the beginning of human history, individuals and societies have attempted to understand destiny through the divine. Natural events that could not be explained were not experienced merely as physical occurrences, but as signs requiring interpretation. This article studies premonition in Western paganism not as an irrational residue of primitive societies, but as an institution situated at the intersection of theology, political authority, collective psychology and decision-making under uncertainty.

The analysis begins with the formation of social norms and the attribution of sacred authority to law. It then examines the plurality of pagan divinities, the Roman institutionalisation of auspices, prodigies and other forms of divination, and the role of sacrifice in preserving the *pax deorum*. The article also considers the transformation, rather than simple disappearance, of divinatory structures under monotheism. Finally, it proposes the concept of **applied theology**: the practical use of a theological worldview to guide action when empirical certainty is unavailable. Premonition did not merely predict the future. It organised fear, legitimised decisions, limited power, created social consensus and offered a language through which human beings could enter into dialogue with the unknown.

## Introduction

Since the beginning of all times, human beings have attempted to explain their destiny from a divine perspective. The incomprehensible phenomena of nature constituted a total enigma for *Homo sapiens*, who, within the immense limits of early knowledge, tried to prophesy or to explain visible events through a sacred meaning. The need to provide an explanation for what we do not understand possesses a fundamental anthropological significance. Agustín Fernández Mallo describes reality as a social “pact” constructed in order to explain what remains unintelligible. Reality is therefore not perceived only as something external and already completed; it is also organised through the concepts, symbols and narratives by which a society makes the world inhabitable.

The social structures that we worship today can be understood more clearly when we observe our ancestors. Modern societies imagine themselves as highly evolved, strict, normative and protected by sophisticated institutions. We may believe that we stand at the summit of the ideal human society. Nothing, however, could be further from the truth. Much of our social model is based on classical patterns that already existed in ancient societies which have now disappeared. Egyptians, Greeks, Romans, Phoenicians, Etruscans, Celts and Germanic peoples, among many others, possessed defined and functioning social orders. Their institutions were not identical to ours, but neither were they shapeless accumulations of superstition.

Every society is structured around an internal normative body. At first, individual human beings exist within families. From the reproductive act there arise groups of persons united through kinship, memory, protection and shared resources. From that point, depending on the culture, families form tribes, clans, villages or broader political communities. In order to prevent permanent conflict among their members, societies develop norms. These norms are initially oral; later, when their complexity increases, they are written and organised into legal or sacred corpora. A *codex* is not the origin of society, but the written form through which an already existing normative order may eventually become fixed, transmitted and enforced.

To provide strength to commandments, prohibitions and obligations, human societies often attribute to them a sphere of divine power. The norms are presented as directives derived from the gods or, in monotheistic societies, from God. Where ancestral authority is predominant, the source of validity may be found not only in mythical beings but also in “the ancients”: the predecessors, founders and lawgivers who existed before the present generation. Divine authority and ancestral authority frequently overlap. The living human being feels entitled to impose law because the law does not appear to originate solely from individual will. It is represented as older, higher and more permanent than the person who applies it.

War emerges when clans, cities or peoples collide over ideology, territory, social organisation, honour or material resources. Within this universe of conflict and uncertainty, premonitions acquire exceptional importance. They were not merely private fantasies. In many ancient societies they formed a true institution. A sign could begin a war, postpone an assembly, interrupt a public action or paralyse an army, depending on whether the favour of the gods was believed to have been obtained or their condemnation revealed.

The central purpose of this article is to analyse these premonitions through what may be called **applied theology**. Theology is usually imagined as abstract reflection on the divine. Applied theology, by contrast, concerns the moment in which a theological conception becomes a practical rule of action. When a magistrate observes the flight of birds before convening an assembly, when a commander interprets lightning before a battle, or when a family reads a dream as a warning, theology ceases to be only a doctrine. It becomes a method of decision.

## 1. Sacred Norms and the Construction of Social Order

The first function of sacred authority is not necessarily to explain the universe in its entirety. Its first practical function is to make obedience possible. A rule pronounced by an ordinary person can be challenged as the expression of a private interest. A rule attributed to the gods, the ancestors or the founders of the community acquires a different character. It appears to stand outside the immediate conflict between individuals.

This does not mean that religion was consciously invented as a deception. Such a reduction would be as simplistic as believing that every sacred rule descended literally and identically from heaven. Institutions normally emerge through long processes in which belief, fear, custom, memory and political interest become inseparable. The divine command may be sincerely believed by the priest, strategically used by the ruler and socially necessary for the community at the same time.

The ancient world did not normally separate religion, politics and law according to modern categories. The sacred calendar determined public time; ritual accompanied political office; family worship reinforced kinship; and war required not only soldiers and weapons but also divine legitimacy. The norm was therefore more than a legal proposition. It was part of a cosmic order.

This point explains why an offence against ritual could be understood as an offence against the entire community. An incorrectly performed sacrifice, an ignored prodigy or an action undertaken under adverse auspices did not concern only the private conscience of the responsible person. It threatened the relationship between the human community and the divine powers on which its stability was believed to depend.

## 2. Pagan Plurality and the Specialisation of the Divine

It is interesting to consider what some describe as the theological impoverishment of monotheism when compared with earlier pagan structures. This expression should not be understood as an objective declaration that monotheism is inferior. It identifies a particular transformation. Polytheistic Mediterranean societies distributed divine action among multiple beings, functions and local cults. Monotheism concentrated ultimate sovereignty in a single God.

The pagan world could assign a divinity, spirit or sacred quality to almost every sphere of life. Agriculture, childbirth, the threshold of the house, commerce, the sea, fire, war, memory and the kitchen could each possess a divine protector or a ritual significance. Roman religion developed this functional tendency with exceptional precision. The result was not simply a chaotic multiplication of gods. It was a theological map of reality.

Through this map, the human being could identify the divine power related to a concrete need. The sailor did not address the sea as an abstract object; the soldier did not understand battle only as a material confrontation; the farmer did not see fertility merely as a mechanical process. Each activity belonged to a network of powers, obligations and possible communications.

The plurality of gods also allowed contradiction to be represented within theology. One divinity could favour war while another protected peace; one could inspire desire while another defended marital order; one could destroy while another healed. Human life contains incompatible impulses, and polytheism gave each impulse a face, a name and a ritual place.

Monotheism did not eliminate these human experiences, but reorganised them. Angels, saints, demons, divine attributes, providence and specialised forms of intercession often occupied functions that, in pagan systems, had been distributed among independent gods. The theological structure changed, but the human demand for mediation remained.

## 3. Premonition as Communication

Premonition must first be distinguished from a simple prediction. A prediction may be produced through observation, experience or calculation. A premonition, in the theological sense studied here, is a sign through which an invisible intelligence is believed to communicate with the visible world.

The sign could be requested or unsolicited. A priest might deliberately consult the gods, while a prodigy could appear without human invitation. The sign could also concern the future, but it did not always describe what would necessarily happen. Frequently, it indicated approval, prohibition, danger or divine displeasure. It answered not only the question “What will occur?” but also “What should we do?”

This distinction is essential. Modern language often reduces divination to fortune-telling. Roman public divination was commonly more practical and political. The question was whether a proposed action possessed divine permission. The answer could be affirmative, negative or ambiguous. The gods were not merely narrators of a fixed future; they were participants in the present decision.

Communication required interpretation. A flash of lightning is physically a meteorological event. Within a sacred system, however, its direction, place, time and relation to a proposed act could transform it into a message. The religious institution did not create the lightning, but it created the language through which lightning could become socially meaningful.

The same applies to birds, dreams, animal behaviour, unusual births, eclipses, earthquakes and sacrificial entrails. Nature became a text. Priests, augurs, seers and interpreters were the readers of that text. Their authority did not rest only on supposed supernatural sensitivity; it also rested on knowledge of inherited rules.

## 4. Greece, Rome and the Institutionalisation of Divination

It would be inaccurate to claim that premonition was exclusive to Rome or that Greece lacked an organised divinatory tradition. Greek religion possessed oracles, inspired prophecy, dream interpretation, sacrificial divination and observation of birds. Delphi alone demonstrates the political and spiritual importance of consultation with the divine in the Greek world.

The distinctively Roman feature was not the invention of divination, but its profound integration into public law, magistracy and state procedure. Rome transformed communication with the gods into an institutional mechanism. Auspices were connected with public authority. Augurs possessed technical and religious knowledge. Prodigies could be reported, evaluated and ritually expiated. The Sibylline Books could be consulted in moments of crisis. Etruscan haruspices interpreted entrails and extraordinary phenomena.

This system created a human structure within the divine. Through a regulated procedure, the ultraterrestrial and the human entered into dialogue. Magistrates and priests asked whether a battle should be undertaken, whether an assembly could proceed, whether a place was appropriate, whether the gods favoured an action or whether ritual correction was required.

The responses could take diverse forms: thunder, lightning, the flight or cry of birds, the behaviour of sacred chickens, the condition of sacrificial organs, dreams, monstrous births or other events classified as prodigies. The important point was not that every unusual event automatically possessed meaning. Meaning arose through a recognised interpretative framework.

Cicero's *De Divinatione* reveals the intellectual tension inside the Roman system. Cicero could defend the political and constitutional importance of augural institutions while subjecting claims of supernatural prediction to severe philosophical criticism. This apparent contradiction is illuminating. An institution can be socially useful even when its metaphysical certainty is disputed.

Roman divination therefore existed between faith and law, conviction and scepticism, divine freedom and political control. Its complexity should prevent us from treating ancient Romans as intellectually inferior. They themselves debated whether signs were genuine, manipulated, misunderstood or preserved because of their value to the state.

## 5. War, Political Power and the Sign of the Gods

The strongest test of applied theology appears in war. A military campaign is an extreme encounter with uncertainty. The commander possesses incomplete information, the soldiers face death, and the political community risks destruction. Under these conditions, a divine sign performs several functions at once.

First, it legitimises action. If the gods favour the campaign, the war is represented not merely as the decision of an ambitious ruler but as an act compatible with cosmic order.

Second, it disciplines the army. Soldiers who believe that the gods support them may possess greater courage and cohesion. An adverse sign, by contrast, may produce fear, hesitation or refusal.

Third, it distributes responsibility. When a decision is attributed partly to divine approval, the human decision-maker no longer carries the entire burden alone. This can protect the leader, but it can also restrain him. A magistrate who must obtain favourable auspices is not theoretically free to act according to pure personal desire.

Fourth, the sign offers a language for stopping. Political systems often find it easier to begin an action than to abandon it without humiliation. An unfavourable omen can provide a sacred reason to postpone, renegotiate or retreat.

For this reason, premonition could begin wars but also prevent them. It could reinforce power but also limit it. The same institution could be used sincerely, manipulated strategically or contested by rival authorities. Its ambiguity was both its strength and its danger.

## 6. Sacrifice, Reciprocity and the *Pax Deorum*

The gods were venerated, and their favour was sought through sacrifice, prayer, vows, libations, incense, plants, food and animal offerings. It would be historically incorrect to describe a simple universal progression from blood sacrifice to harmless organic offerings. Different forms coexisted for long periods, and animal sacrifice remained central to many ancient cults. What changed depended on time, place, social group and religious transformation.

Roman ritual was often structured through reciprocity. Human beings offered something to the gods and expected protection, fertility, victory, health or restoration of order. This relationship is sometimes summarised by the formula *do ut des*: I give so that you may give. The formula should not reduce Roman religion to a crude commercial contract. It expresses the idea that relations between humans and gods were maintained through reciprocal obligation.

The broader objective was the *pax deorum*, the peace or harmonious relationship between the community and the gods. A prodigy could indicate that this relationship had been disturbed. Ritual expiation attempted to restore balance. Premonition and sacrifice were therefore connected: the sign diagnosed a rupture, and ritual action treated it.

From an applied-theological perspective, sacrifice transformed uncertainty into procedure. The community could not control storms, epidemics or military disaster, but it could perform a recognised action. Ritual did not necessarily remove danger; it converted passive fear into collective response.

This is one reason why sacrifice cannot be understood only as cruelty or irrational waste. It was a social language of value. The offering demonstrated that the community considered its relationship with the divine sufficiently important to surrender something meaningful. The cost of the offering made the prayer visible.

## 7. Monotheism: Suppression, Transformation and Continuity

The triumph of Christianity did not simply eliminate prophecy, signs or the desire to know divine will. It transformed the categories through which they were judged. Christian authorities distinguished legitimate prophecy, providential signs and divine inspiration from condemned divination, magic and pagan consultation. The boundary was real, but it was not always clear.

Prayer, visions, dreams, saints, relics, the interpretation of Scripture and the conviction that “God wills it” preserved a practical relationship between invisible authority and human action. Late antique and medieval Christians continued to seek knowledge about illness, travel, war, marriage and destiny, although the legitimate source of such knowledge was increasingly required to be the Christian God rather than pagan gods or illicit spirits.

The difference was theological and institutional. Pagan pluralism allowed multiple divine agents and local systems of consultation. Christianity placed all legitimate supernatural knowledge under the sovereignty of one God and under the interpretative authority of the Church. Practices were not judged only by what they did, but by the power from which they were believed to come.

Judaism likewise contains prophecy, dreams, providence and sacred interpretation while prohibiting various forms of divination. Islam distinguishes revelation and lawful trust in God from forbidden attempts to obtain hidden knowledge through illicit means. In each case, the human desire remains similar: to know whether a decision corresponds to a higher order. What changes is the authorised channel.

It is therefore more accurate to speak of **translation** than disappearance. The old question—“Do the gods favour this act?”—becomes “Is this the will of God?”, “Is this event providential?”, or “Has a sign been given?” Pagan premonition survives not necessarily as pagan doctrine, but as a structure of religious consciousness.

## 8. The Psychological and Anthropological Necessity of Premonition

This tradition is often described today as absurd, foolish or primitive. In reality, it has several explanations, especially when analysed from psychological and anthropological perspectives. Even without deciding whether a premonition is metaphysically true or functional, we can understand why it was, *de facto*, necessary.

Human beings suffer when required to choose under radical uncertainty. A decision creates multiple possible futures, but only one can be lived. The greater the consequences, the greater the anxiety. Divination converts an open and terrifying future into an interpretable sign.

The sign does not always provide certainty. Often it provides a direction. It tells the person when to wait, when to act, what danger to avoid or which ritual obligation must be fulfilled. In this sense, divination operates as a technology of decision-making. Its instruments are symbolic rather than statistical, but its practical problem is recognisable: how can action be justified when complete knowledge is impossible?

Premonition also externalises inner conflict. A person divided between two choices may be unable to recognise his own preference. The oracle, dream or omen returns the conflict in an external form. The answer can then be accepted, rejected or interpreted. Even resistance to the sign reveals something about the subject.

At the collective level, the institution creates consensus. A group that would otherwise divide over a decision may accept the result of an authorised ritual. The divine sign functions as a third authority above the factions. It may prevent violence precisely because neither side appears to surrender directly to the other.

This does not prove the supernatural origin of the sign. It explains its social efficacy. A practice may be psychologically useful without being metaphysically true; it may also be metaphysically true without being interpreted correctly. Applied theology must keep these possibilities distinct.

## 9. Three Interpretations of the Premonition

A rigorous study should consider at least three interpretations.

### 9.1. Genuine divine communication

According to the theological interpretation, the sign is produced, selected or made meaningful by a divine intelligence. The bird, storm or dream is not necessarily contrary to natural law. The divine may act through natural causality rather than against it. The premonition is therefore visible in its effect while its ultimate cause remains invisible.

This interpretation cannot ordinarily be demonstrated through the same methods used to establish a physical mechanism. Its claim concerns intention and meaning, not merely material sequence.

### 9.2. Psychological and symbolic projection

According to the psychological interpretation, the sign allows unconscious fears, desires and intuitions to become conscious. The mind selects or organises external events according to an internal conflict. The premonition is true not because it predicts an objective future, but because it reveals the state of the person or community interpreting it.

This explanation does not make the experience irrelevant. A symbol created or selected by the mind can transform conduct, identity and memory.

### 9.3. Political and institutional construction

According to the political interpretation, divination is a mechanism of authority. Priests and magistrates control the interpretation of signs and can use them to legitimise or obstruct action. An omen may become a constitutional instrument, a weapon between factions or a language through which elite conflict is regulated.

These interpretations are not always mutually exclusive. A Roman magistrate could sincerely believe in Jupiter, experience genuine psychological relief and obtain political advantage from the same auspice. Human institutions rarely possess only one cause.

## 10. The Danger of Manipulation

The capacity to interpret the invisible gives power. Whoever defines the sign can influence the action that follows. This danger was recognised in antiquity itself.

Ambiguous signs are especially vulnerable to manipulation. A ruler may seek repeated consultation until receiving the desired answer. A priest may interpret the same phenomenon differently according to political allegiance. An unfavourable omen may be concealed, while a useful prodigy is publicly magnified.

The invocation of divine will can also remove moral responsibility. “The god commanded it” or “God wills it” may convert human ambition into sacred necessity. War, persecution and sacrifice can then be protected from criticism because opposition appears not merely political but impious.

Applied theology must therefore include an ethics of interpretation. No person should obtain unlimited authority merely by claiming access to the divine. A sign that demands injustice cannot be accepted solely because it is presented as supernatural. The invisible must not become a refuge for the irresponsible exercise of visible power.

At the same time, complete dismissal is not a solution. To declare every sacred sign absurd may conceal the fact that modern societies also construct authoritative systems for acting under uncertainty. The problem is not whether humans interpret signs. The problem is how those interpretations are tested, limited and made accountable.

## 11. Premonition and Modernity

Modern societies claim to have replaced omens with data. In many fields this is true and represents an extraordinary advance. Meteorology explains storms; medicine studies disease; statistics estimates risk; and military intelligence analyses material conditions. These methods possess an empirical foundation fundamentally different from ancient divination and should not be confused with it.

Nevertheless, the underlying human situation has not disappeared. Data cannot eliminate every uncertainty. A probability is not a personal destiny. A forecast can describe what usually occurs without answering what a particular individual should do in a unique moment.

Modern people therefore continue to search for signs. They interpret coincidences, dreams, intuitions, repeated numbers, unexpected encounters and moments of apparent convergence. Religious persons may call them providence. Secular persons may call them instinct, synchronicity or chance. The vocabulary changes more easily than the anthropological need.

Even institutional forecasting can acquire an oracular aura when its technical limits are forgotten. Economic models, electoral projections and algorithmic recommendations may be treated as if they spoke with absolute authority. The danger is similar to ancient manipulation: the interpreter hides behind the supposed neutrality of the sign.

The lesson of pagan premonition is not that modern science and ancient divination are equivalent. They are not. The lesson is that every society develops procedures for converting uncertainty into action, and every procedure can become sacred when its authority is no longer questioned.

## 12. Applied Theology

The concept of applied theology makes it possible to unite the previous analysis. Theology is applied when an idea of divine order produces a concrete decision, institution or pattern of conduct.

Premonition is applied theology because it establishes:

1. **a cosmology**, according to which the universe is capable of carrying meaning;
2. **an anthropology**, according to which the human being can receive or interpret that meaning;
3. **an institution**, which determines who may interpret signs and according to which rules;
4. **a decision**, through which interpretation becomes action;
5. **a ritual consequence**, intended to preserve or restore the relationship with the divine.

This model explains why premonition was more than superstition. It connected the structure of the cosmos with the structure of government. It joined the intimate fear of the individual with the public decisions of the city. It transformed theology into administration.

The model also reveals the decisive question: does the institution remain open to divine freedom, or does it merely use the divine name to confirm human desire? A genuine dialogue cannot exist if the gods are permitted to say only what the ruler wishes to hear.

Theologically, therefore, an unfavourable sign may be more important than a favourable one. It demonstrates that the divine is not simply an instrument of political will. Institutionally, the possibility of refusal gives the ritual its authority. If every consultation produces consent, consultation becomes theatre.

## Conclusions

Premonitions in Western paganism were not marginal curiosities. They were institutions through which ancient societies organised the relationship between visible events and invisible order. They could guide war, regulate assemblies, justify sacrifice, restore communal confidence and limit or reinforce political power.

The ancient person did not inhabit a dead universe composed only of objects. Nature spoke. Lightning, birds, dreams, entrails, eclipses and prodigies could become parts of a sacred language. The priest or magistrate did not merely observe nature; he interpreted the possible intention within it.

Theological plurality gave pagan societies an extensive map of divine functions. Roman religion transformed parts of that map into public procedure. The result was a sophisticated form of applied theology in which divine approval became relevant to law and political action.

Monotheism did not completely destroy this structure. It concentrated divine authority, condemned many pagan techniques and reclassified the legitimate channels of supernatural knowledge. Prophecy, providence, prayer, dreams and sacred interpretation continued to answer the same human need: to determine whether action corresponds to a higher order.

From the psychological perspective, premonition responds to anxiety and uncertainty. From the anthropological perspective, it produces consensus and social meaning. From the political perspective, it legitimises and restrains power. From the theological perspective, it makes possible a dialogue between the human being and an intelligence that cannot be directly possessed.

None of these explanations alone is sufficient. To reduce every omen to fraud ignores belief; to reduce it to psychology ignores institutions; to affirm every sign as divine ignores manipulation; and to dismiss the entire phenomenon as idiocy ignores its historical function.

The final conclusion is therefore not that ancient premonitions must be accepted as empirically demonstrated truths. Nor is it that they should be abandoned as meaningless errors. Their importance lies in the structure they reveal. Human beings require a language for uncertainty. They need to act before possessing complete knowledge, and they seek an authority capable of connecting the fragile present with an intelligible future.

Western paganism placed that authority in a plurality of gods and signs. Monotheism placed it under the sovereignty of one God. Modernity increasingly places it in scientific prediction, institutional expertise and individual intuition. The forms change, but the question remains: when the future is invisible and action cannot be postponed, what allows the human being to decide?

Premonition was one ancient answer. Its enduring value lies not only in whether the sign was true, but in the fact that, through the sign, the human being attempted to transform fear into order, uncertainty into ritual and silence into dialogue.
